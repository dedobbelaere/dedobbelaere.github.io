I have been playing a lot of backgammon lately. A few times a week, I'll recruit my roommate to play a match to eleven with me. Outside of that, I spend many free hours playing against an engine on my phone. When I was in Montreal, I brought my board and taught anyone I could how to play. It has gotten to the point that when I close my eyes to go to sleep at night, I see checkers moving around the board.

I am no stranger to obsession. If I was, I wouldn't be in graduate school. Pursuing a Ph.D. in philosophy requires a slightly neurotic obsession with a rather narrow area of a particular field of study, sustained over a period of 5-7 years, with very little in the way of external incentives to pursue this field of study. Many people in academia, I'm sure, will sympathize with this feeling. Some questions just grab you, and don't let go until you've written an entire book or more on the subject.

But why backgammon? What could possibly motivate an obsession with something so trivial? At least when a question in philosophy grabs me, there is some sense in which I am pursuing the big questions, things that have puzzled humans for a significant chunk of our history and which have some, ostensibly, some utility to answering. Backgammon is not deep in this way. There is no further substance to the game beyond the game itself. There are ways to play the game better and worse, but little else which might serve to further our understanding of the human condition.

I'm not expecting my board games to contain the answers to these deeper questions. Rather, I am puzzled at how something so trivial can motivate such intense focus and study. The game is not as simple as, say, tic-tac-toe, which anyone can learn to play perfectly within the course of an afternoon. But it is not as deep as chess. A person who learns backgammon as an adult can get to a relatively high level of competency in backgammon in just a few years, or even months with enough dedication, while a person who learns chess as an adult is far less likely to ever become a serious player.

Some amount of my obsession, I'm inclined to think, is caused by the same sort of process that gets people addicted to slot machines or endless scrolling. There is an element of chance in the game, and it is easy to get a little dopamine hit from getting the right roll at the right time. When playing against a computer, there is also an element of endless availability. I can play a game whenever I want, the games are relatively quick, and as soon as I'm done with a game the application I play on moves onto the next one.

This still isn't a complete explanation. Despite my generally addictive personality I find it pretty easy to refrain from scrolling on social media, and I am rarely online for more than an hour or so a week, usually to check messages and make my weekly blog announcement posts. I'm not sucked in by everything that offers me instant gratification, and in fact my other hobbies (reading, learning a second language) require an attunement to delayed gratification on the scale of weeks to years.

It certainly helps that I'm good at backgammon. I attribute this mostly to playing Sorry! growing up, which like backgammon involves racing to move one's pieces around a board while trying to knock your opponents back to their starting place. I beat my roommate more often than not, and can maintain a strong performance rating (determined by engine evaluation) throughout a match. I'm not competing for the world championship (yet), but I can enjoy the feeling of competence.

Yet this isn't a complete explanation on its own either. It is a stronger explanation when combined with the naturally addictive features of the game, but still there is something missing. Why this thing? For the same reasons I could be developing an obsession with chess, or even with online poker. There is something missing in the complete accounting of why this game in particular has captured my attention so thoroughly.

I could speculate further, but I think the ultimate answer is going to be that there isn't really a complete explanation, at least not one that can be easily attached to language. Instead, I think the best thing to appeal to in order to complete this explanation is the unconscious. There is something, some process, below my conscious awareness in my brain, which in some way found a thing in the world that brought it enjoyment, and responded accordingly.

I don't often plan these blog posts. When I write public philosophy, where my goal is to explain some philosophical concept, I plan it out a bit and think carefully about the best way to communicate the general outlines of the problem in such a way that my readers will be able to understand it. Otherwise, I just sit down on Sunday afternoon and write whatever comes to mind. I let my mind wander, and put it all out onto the page.

I find this process particularly therapeutic, and I think the reason why is that it gives me the opportunity to sort through the things in my life that I haven't yet attached to language, that I haven't yet really examined critically. Attaching these subconscious things to language is what enables me to change understand and, if necessary, change my relationship to them.

So with backgammon, it doesn't really do much to just say "it's the subconscious!" and move on, there needs to be some further attempt to match language to the phenomenon of my obsession.

My best attempt is this. I like using my brain, and I like the feeling of winning at something that requires me to use my brain. I don't have much opportunity to do that in my professional life, because I have very carefully tried to make my relationship to philosophy one that is more collaborative than competitive, a project which has since undergrad, I think, been largely successful. So when I find an outlet where I can win with some regularity and use my brain doing so, it brings me the same sort of enjoyment that I used to get from outmaneuvering some other undergrad in the seminar room when I started doing philosophy. For whatever reason, that species of enjoyment appeals to me, and so I find myself spending a lot of time playing backgammon.

There are other games out there that could provide a similar service, but backgammon just so happened to be there. I had a board, my roommate and I like to teach each other new games every now and again, and so one night we brought it out and I found that I could get this enjoyment out of it. I don't find myself getting the same enjoyment out of chess because my relative strength at chess is far lower than my relative strength in backgammon.

I changed my competitive relationship with philosophy into a more collaborative one because I found that philosophy is just ill-suited for competition. It is a far more rewarding subject when approached as a collective endeavor, rather than a high-school debate competition. Backgammon doesn't quite make sense as a collective endeavor in the same way. Someone wins and someone loses. I don't intend to start taking the kumbaya approach to backgammon anytime soon. But perhaps I could gloat a bit less when I beat my roommate, and perhaps in being aware of the competitive urge that drives much of my enjoyment, I can shift the locus of that enjoyment from winning to enjoying playing with other people.

On the other hand, "backgammon world champion" would be a nice line on my resume.
