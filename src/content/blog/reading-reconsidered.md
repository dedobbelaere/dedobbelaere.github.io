Helen DeWitt's *magnum opus* is *The Last Samurai*, a novel that shares a title and nothing else in common with the Tom Cruise movie. *The Last Samurai* follow Sibylla, a polyglot, single mother, and inefficacious breadwinner, and her prodigious son Ludo, who masters Greek, Arabic, linear algebra, and fluid mechanics by the age of six. Ludo is without a father figure, and spends the latter half of the book, inspired by the recruitment process in Kurosawa's *Seven Samurai*, testing potential paternal patrons. Each of these suitors is a public intellectual in some way, and Ludo tests them by contriving some reason to meet with them, then telling each man he is his son. A good samurai will parry the blow.

The book's primary thesis is that a life of intellectual stimulation is both accessible and valuable. Ludo is a child prodigy, but he sees himself as more akin to John Stuart Mill than Ramanujan. Ramanujan was an Indian mathematics prodigy who, by all appearances, seems to simply have had a stellar natural aptitude for the subject. Mill, on the other hand, came to genius by nurture rather than nature. He writes in his autobiography about seeing himself as being of average or below-average intelligence, but that his poor natural aptitude was outweighed the advantages of a strong education.

DeWitt is, I think, an optimist. She, or at least Sibylla, sees in each of us a potential John Stuart Mill. Her frustration with the world outside of the intelligentsia appears to be borne out of the inability of others to recognize their latent intellectual potential. When Sibylla interacts with a character who doesn't appreciate the value of, say, reading the Odyssey in the original Greek *beyond* the instrumental value of learning Greek root words, she reacts with mild bewilderment that this person cannot see the non-instrumental value of the work. She doesn't insult them, she doesn't deride them. When she comes to a disagreement with someone she will do her best to persuade them with a rational argument (though, choosing strange hills for mortal combat, she often fails).

I have made absolutely clear, in multiple previous posts, that I am a snob. I am of the persuasion that learning something outside of one's trade, or reading serious literature, or learning a new language, or learning a new language to read serious literature in that language, are all activities of higher value than, say, doomscrolling, or reading smut, or trying to digest the entire deluge of streaming content now available. In general, I think that there are some ways a person can spend their time that are better than others.

Of course, the latter claim is far less contentious than the former. Most people would agree (I hope) that it is better to spend three hours after work reading a book rather than smashing one's head against a wall. Where my position becomes radical, by the standards of our time, is in refusing to concede that it is good for people to read *anything*. I've discussed this before <a href="https://dedobb.xyz/blog/all-reading-or-good-reading/" target="_blank">here</a>, coming to the conclusion that it's fine to read unserious literature, but only as a means to an end. By analogy, walking is valuable to build up stamina when doing the Couch to 5k program, but that doesn't mean that one should keep walking once the necessary stamina to start running has been cultivated.

There is something more to be gained from reading Helen DeWitt than from Sarah J. Maas. A book like *The Last Samurai* doesn't merely entertain. It facilitates the communication of complex ideas and concepts that simply can't be boiled down to a more basic form. It can have a profound effect on one's character and dispositions, and it can do so while pushing the English language beyond its quotidian utilitarian patterns of expression and into rapturously beautiful usage.

I am rather vocal about this sentiment, and I receive plenty of counter-sentiments. Before I respond to these counter-sentiments, I want to make one thing very, very clear. I do not hold this view because I think that it is better to be a member of an elite class that lords over illiterate proles. I hold this view because I find extended, concerted intellectual efforts to be among the most valuable things in my life. I, like Sibylla, do not think that the use of higher cognition is reserved for any specific class of people. Anyone is capable of sophisticated intellectual activity, it is just a matter of making the effort. I'll begin with the weaker arguments I've been presented with, and move up to the stronger ones.

**Counter-point 1:** *The unserious literature you're talking about is mostly romantasy and mostly produced and consumed by women, this is just thinly veiled misogyny. Let women have fun!*

I think this point is actually more regressive than it attempts to make me out to be, because it presupposes that women either aren't writing serious literature or aren't fit to enjoy serious literature. Both points which I think are obviously false. Helen DeWitt (duh), Olga Tokarczuk, Jhumpa Lahiri, Han Kang, Charlotte Wood, all serious, all brilliant, all well beyond my skill with a keyboard. I also, of course, have no doubt in my mind that any woman is just as capable of enjoying serious literature as any man. Case in point: my parents. My father reads almost exclusively pulp fiction spy novels, and my mother reads almost exclusively literary fiction. My appreciation for the novel had to come from someone, right?

**Counter-point 2:** *People need to be able to relax and turn their brain off, expecting people to be using their full cognitive capacity all of the time is unreasonable. Let people have fun!*

Yes, everyone needs rest, and everyone runs out of steam. How long it takes one to run out of steam, however, it a matter of conditioning and careful variation. There is only so long that I can spend reading dense, technical philosophy before I simply cannot take any more for the day. Exhausting my stamina for philosophy, however, doesn't necessarily exhaust my stamina for other intellectual activities. I can take a 20-30 minute walk home from the office, maybe get dinner, and then jump into reading a novel or practicing a language or playing chess. It also helps that I've conditioned my brain to be able to handle more and more strain each day. Yes, if you go straight from your couch to running five miles a day, you're going to completely exhaust yourself. Once you build up a base level of endurance, however, the extra daily load is entirely manageable.

Furthermore, why must using one's brain always be seen as work? In the words of David Foster Wallace (remember I said I was a snob): reading is fun! I'm not advocating for people to make themselves miserable, only that they find more rewarding ways to spend their time. General masochism is not the goal here. Intellectual pursuits can be both demanding and enjoyable. Again, athletics provide a useful analogy: working out *hurts*, but that doesn't mean it isn't enjoyable. Arguably, intellectual challenges aren't even painful, just energy- and focus-intensive.

**Counter-point 3:** *Life is hard enough, what grounds do you have to tell other people how to spend their free time? Let people live!*

I'm writing a blog post, not drafting legislation. I wouldn't force the sorts of experiences and habits for which I'm advocating here on anyone. Partly because it would be wrong to override another person's agency in that way, partly because I don't think that would lead to any real appreciation for intellectual goods. My goal, instead, is to persuade. I have gotten immense value from changing the focus of my leisure time, and I think that other people would find that they stand to gain a great deal from doing the same.

I perhaps make some assumptions about value. I do not have direct access to the Platonic form of the good, and it is possible that I am wrong about where value inheres in our world. My own experiences suggest to me that this is not the case. I find myself enjoying my free time more when I spend it on something that contributes to my mental development. Those returns are multiplied when I share them with others.

The death of reading in America is more and more a concern about the public welfare and the health of our democracy (see *The Atlantic*'s August cover story). But improved literacy is not (merely) a chore of democracy. It is not reserved for a rarified elite. My goal is not to shame you if you watch hours of reality television every day. I only want to make you aware of the opportunity in front of you. There are higher pleasures out there, and they are yours for the taking.
