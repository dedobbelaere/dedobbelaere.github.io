I, like everyone, make decisions that affect other people. Sometimes these decisions are significant. The starting or ending of relationships, romantic or otherwise, making or breaking promises, etc. Sometimes these decisions are not so significant. Recently, I made the not so significant decision to change which course I am enrolling in for the fall semester, for my last classroom credit required for my Ph.D.

I was originally enrolled in a course on the philosophy of education. Education is a topic near and dear to me. My late maternal grandfather, one of my biggest supporters in pursuing post-graduate education, was a high school teacher, later principal, later superintendent, at a school district in rural western Illinois. He would talk with gentle pride, decades after leaving his role, about the computer lab he had secured for his students or the creative accounting he used to ensure that everyone had the resources they needed to teach and learn effectively. Reading about education and its purposes helps me to feel close to him.

But I ended up changing my enrollment to a course in the philosophy of language. I am less excited about the philosophy of language. I've always found it abstruse even by the standards of analytic philosophy. I am, however, a metaphysician, and to do metaphysics effectively in this day and age one must have a solid grasp of the philosophy of language. So I decided it would be best for me to brush up.

The particulars of this decision are less important, at least in these pages, than the subsequent guilt I feel towards the professor teaching the course in philosophy of education and my late grandfather. I've had a number of talks about the philosophy of education with the professor in his office hours, and I've read some papers on the subject in my free hours to discuss with him. I also know that if my grandfather were around he would be overjoyed to see my interest in education.

There is a part of me that feels as though I've let them both down by switching my course registration. I feel as though I misled the professor with my interest in the subject and by demonstrating such enthusiasm for the course material. Of course, I didn't mislead him. That interest and enthusiasm is real, and I would still love to audit the course when I have some free time in my day. I just found that the best decision for me at this time is to take philosophy of language.

I have this problem often. I feel like I can't make a decision that affects someone else unless I can give them reasons that they would accept for making that decision. Even if I have reasons that they could accept, as with this professor who would probably agree that philosophy of language is a valuable course for me to be taking, there is some part of me that struggles to attach a positive valence to decisions that I make to meet my *own* needs or pursue my *own* goals.

It's not that I find myself in a constant state of self-sacrifice. Rather, I know the decision I want to make, the decision that would be best for me, and I agonize over finding a way to justify it such that the person(s) who might be affected by that decision would be happy for me to make it. Usually I just make the decision anyways and take on some greater-than-proportional guilt for the decision made. As is the case with the philosophy of education class. I have no strong reasons for guilt, yet here I am writing 1000+ words about decision-making because of it.

I think much of this is connected to another anxiety that I have, which is about making the correct choices. Every choice must be the one that *will* advance my goals, not the one that is merely *likely* to advance my goals or *rational to choose* given my total information. In short I require of my decisions that I know their outcome in advance. Which is absurd. I am demanding of myself omniscience.

I'm not totally ignorant of this tendency. I haven't come to know this about myself just through writing this blog post on Sunday afternoon. I'm trying to shift my focus elsewhere. To not evaluate my decisions based on what the actual outcomes are or whether or not I could justify them to someone else but rather evaluating them by whether or not I could live with them.

This can be dangerous. Taken too far it could amount to radical moral subjectivism. So long as *I* find my actions acceptable, then it doesn't matter if they're right or wrong, right? Wrong. It's right that in the wrong hands this system might render right wrong and wrong right. It's slightly frightening, making light of wrong and right.

I should make my point before I get carried away. Yes, someone who has no moral grounding whatsoever, living as if all that matters is whether or not they can live with their decisions, would probably act like a sociopath. I've been studying ethics for years, and for my entire life before that I had the moral principles of the Catholic church firmly inculcated into the bedrock of my psyche. I'm not a moral saint, but I'm certainly not at risk of sociopathy.

I couldn't live with a decision that was deeply immoral. So, I can trust that I wouldn't make those sorts of decisions if I'm using a subjective standard to evaluate what I do. I also know that any symbolic system will have its limits. In morally fraught situations, it's probably best to think in terms of right and wrong rather than what can be tolerated.

What this system yields for me is not better decision making, in the sense of maximizing expected value. It's no way to approach a game of poker. What I do get is a better relationship with the desire for every decision to be correct. It's much easier to anticipate whether or not a decision will be something I can live with, than what it's actual effects will be. 

One might think that whether or not I can live with a decision is wholly determined by what its effects are. This is obviously not how I evaluate whether or not I can live with a decision, otherwise I wouldn't have gotten anywhere by adopting this new system. The most important factor in determining whether or not I can live with a decision is whether or not the decision reflects what I truly, authentically want *at the time the decision is made*. 

This is not an easy task. It requires very careful assessment of the decision at hand and my own values, and the diligent sifting out of the inauthentic influences that might push me towards a decision I don't actually want to make. It doesn't mean I need to make decisions that will comport with my values over the entire course of my life. This wouldn't get me very far, as I would be trying to predict all of the values I might plausibly adopt over the course of my life, and trying to please all these different value sets.

For example, when I was in high school, I made a lot of really stupid choices. Excessive drinking, light streetracing, attempted genocide of neurons by means of janky dab cartridges. Unfortunately, these were all decisions in line with what I wanted at the time. They are decisions that I regret, but that I can live with. I know what I was like at the time and I can't fault that reckless teenager for making the decisions that his underdeveloped brain wasn't equipped to evaluate as I would now.

There is one thing that's certain, and that is that I'll have to live with myself as long as I keep living. I'm stuck in my own head after all. I'm still uncertain whether the decisions I make are the best ones. That doesn't go away. But I can get a more stable relationship with that uncertainty, it doesn't have to keep me up at night. I can be uncertain, but I can live with it.
