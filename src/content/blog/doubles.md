It is no easy task to know what could have been. There has been a sizable amount of work in contemporary analytic philosophy dedicated to understanding what constitutes knowledge about what could have been. Very little progress, in my opinion, has been made. The best answer we seem to have is that conceivability, the ability to have a clear and distinct idea of a counterfactual state of affairs, is a good enough guide to possibility.

But conceivability provides us with rather weak warrant for believing in other possible worlds. I don't think that the fact that I can conceive of a possible world in which I wore a red shirt rather than a blue shirt today takes necessitarianism, the view that there is only one possible world, the actual world, off the table.

In any case, we only get one timeline to live through. We don't get to live through each possible consequence of our actions, we only get to see one way that things turn out. We can turn over in our minds the possibilities, the questions of what we might have done and who we might have become, but we don't get to know. We only get to guess.

Jon Fosse's *Septology*, one of the finest works of literature published in the past century, places its main character in direct contact with another possible self. The novel follows Asle, a painter living in coastal Norway, who at one point rescues his alcoholic friend Asle, also a painter, who he finds passed out in the snow. The two Asles are variations on a life, the first finds religion and some measure of peace, while the second finds no such light.

I do not know any other Adams who so neatly serve as my doppelganger. Sometimes, in the right light, I catch shades of what I could have been. I'll see a crowd of twenty-somethings, perfectly groomed and draped in athleisure, moving between bars on a Saturday afternoon, and I think of that frat boy whose life overlapped with mine so many years ago. There is a world where I never quit drinking, where I didn't drop my second major in economics, where I joined the ranks of some consulting firm or tech giant, where I remained a weekend warrior.

I see it in cities. The commutes I might've had, the entranceways that might've been mine, the people I might have fallen in love with, the windows that might've let me look out onto the street rather than into someone else's life. What if, what if, what if. Would I still feel like myself? Would I be me? Does inhabiting this subjective perspective require making the same way through space-time?

"Although, of course, you end up becoming yourself" sez DFW. I get conceivability and little else. Would things be better if I could choose which self to inhabit? Some way to wake up every day and choose which Adam DeDobbelaere best suits me today. I'm inclined to think any way to flesh the idea out results in nonsense. 

I might inherit everything about this other Adam, including long and short term memories, in which case I don't even remember who I was before taking the new perspective, defeating the point. I might take on all their memories along with my own, in which case I'm not really getting life from their perspective, just adding something to my own perspective. Or I don't get any of their memories in which case I'm just doing something different from my usual routine.

I fear this piece might give the impression that I spend a lot of time wishing I could be someone else. Sometimes, surely, though rarely. I'm glad to be done with drinking, and to be pursuing academia rather than the clawing my way to the top of the corporate dogpile. I like my friends, I like my roommates. Rochester has grown on me. Not enough to keep me here any longer than it takes to complete the Ph.D., but enough to enjoy the days I spend off-campus.

I still have to make decisions every day about who to become. About which habits to continue, which to discontinue, about my dispositions and my clothes and my manner of speech. I don't get to live other lives but I choose between them constantly. Am I going to be the Adam that wore a red shirt today or the Adam that wore a blue shirt?

These choices don't always neatly match up against each other. From an economist's perspective, everything can be measured against willingness to pay. Philosophers don't always think this way. Incommensurable goods are goods of the sort that can't be compared to each other apples to apples. There's no amount of money you could offer me that would compel me to choose a life where I couldn't do philosophy or read fiction. It's not that the price I attach to philosophy and literature is infinite, it's that they're different sorts of goods, and trying to exchange one for the other is a category error.

I don't often find myself measuring up against Adams who have chosen to pursue the same sort of goods, but who have been more effective in accumulating them. I have no doubt that there are Adams out there in possibility-space who are getting their Ph.D. at more prestigious institutions, who are publishing more than I am or ever will, and who do better philosophy than I do. Some of them might even write better blogs. These other selves are not terribly interesting to think about, except when I need the motivation to spend a bit more time editing a paper or take slightly more careful notes when reading a piece for one of my seminars. Dwelling on them beyond that is, I think, thinly veiled masochism.

I find it much more interesting to consider the Adams who have chosen to pursue different sorts of goods. Are they happy? Are they fulfilled? Do they wish they read more? I've spent this entire piece thinking about doppelgangers and inhabiting other possible selves, but in all truth I can't imagine what it's like to be one of these more distant Adams. I'm almost surprised by any claims they have on being me and vice versa. I imagine a young man, about six foot one, mustache, buzz cut, whose nose has freckles like mine and whose eyes match my dull green. Superficially, we might be the same, but I can't imagine we would find each other's company comforting.

I would be in a sorry state, I think, if I found myself sympathizing with those other Adams or chasing after what they have. I hope they're happy, and I hope they don't find themselves thinking about me too often. I'd probably still encourage them to read more if they haven't been lately. Some of them are waiting for me, in 20 years or so. We'll get to know each other along the way. You end up becoming yourself, after all.
