As I said above, I've been in Montreal for the past week, staying in a hostel near the city center. One of the blessings of hostel life is the potent social atmosphere. Everyone is happy to chat, and there is someone from everywhere. So I've been meeting people, hanging out, giving intro to philosophy lessons to all the people who haven't met a flesh-and-blood philosopher before, etc.

One of these people is going through an interesting transformative experience. Keeping it to the broad strokes to protect their privacy, this person grew up in and is still somewhat attached to a conservative religious community, has recently become disenchanted, and is in the process of sorting out the extent to which they want to continue to associate with this community and participate in religious life. Thank God they are, because I wanted to talk about decision-making anyways this week and I needed an appropriate segue.

The use of the words "transformative experience" is not merely one of my usual sagacious textual choices. There is a wide philosophical literature on transformative experiences and the problems that they pose. Roughly, a transformative experience is one in which the person making the decision either lacks any way to know what an experience will be like before it happens (like falling in love for the first time) or will come out on the other side with key features of their self having been significantly altered (as with becoming a parent).

Oftentimes a transformative experience involves some mix of both epistemic and personal transformation, as with this person I met in Montreal. They weren't sure what their life was going to be like on the other side, how they would feel about shifting the relationship they stand in to their faith, or the extent to which their faith would remain relevant to them. Nor were they certain about what kind of person they might be on the other side, what sort of preferences and long-term goals they might have, and how they might evaluate the decision in retrospect.

Transformative experiences, though occasionally dramatic, are everywhere. When you go to a coffee shop and order a drink you've never tried before, you're having a transformative experience. You can't really know whether you'll like that drink or what the subjective experience of its flavor will be before you take a sip.

There's always the option to play it safe. Stick with the things you know, or the things that are sufficiently close to the things that you know such that their phenomenal experience can be reliably predicted. Of course, if we all did this for our entire lives then we'd all be trying to survive on a diet of exclusively breast milk. I imagine this would be a dietary and logistical nightmare.

One way to think about making such transformative decisions is in terms of calculating expected value. Even though we can't know precisely what the experience is like, we can rely on testimony from others who we trust and who have been through a similar transformative experience to give us some sense of the relative weights of the various options and act accordingly.

This works for some transformative experiences, especially the minor ones, like choosing a new tea blend to taste. If I know that I like Earl Grey tea, and a friend with similar tastes of mine also likes Earl Grey, and they recommend English breakfast to me, I can be reasonably sure that I'll like English breakfast. This approach tends to break down when we consider bigger transformative experiences like becoming a parent. 

It doesn't really make sense for me to rely on the testimony of others who have become parents, because no matter how much like me they are, the experience of becoming a parent is so radical, so total, and so varied, that it might beget wildly different experiences, to the extent that testimony can only give us a shade of a shade of what the full spectrum of the experience is like.

The problem maybe isn't as difficult as I'm making it out to be, or at least as I perceive it to be. People fall in love, get married, have children, usually without any help from the philosophical perspectives available on transformative experiences. 

In July I wrote about making decisions that I can live with. This doesn't quite work with transformative experiences, as trying to make a decision that I can live with still involves projecting myself into the future and considering whether I think I'll be the sort of person, or whether I'll have the sort of experience, that I can live with. But if I don't really know what sort of person I'll be on the other side or what sort of experience I'll have, this projection somewhat falls apart.

I also talked about authenticity, and making decisions that comport with values that are deeply held and thoroughly reflected upon. I'm frankly not sure whether this helps with transformative experiences either. I can reflect on my values and make a decision accordingly, but after the transformative experience my values might have shifted, or I might find out through the experience that it actually doesn't align with my values in the way I previously thought. Perhaps I can take solace in the fact that I made the best decision I could at the time under conditions of extreme uncertainty, but this is a consolation prize at best.

Every time I have fallen in love has been a radically different experience. Each loving relationship I have had has affected me differently, operated at a different frequency or magnitude, and left a different imprint on my psyche. After it passes, or fades, or abruptly repatriates itself from its seat in the soul back to the distant world of forms, I face the question of whether or not to do it again. I keep giving it another shot. Why take the risk? Why put myself in the line of fire?

I'm writing this in a coffee shop in Montreal. I'm drink more tea than coffee these days. My pot just ran out. It's a blend I haven't tried before. I liked it. I think I'm going to try their cake next. Why not?
