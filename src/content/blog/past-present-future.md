A few weeks ago I wrote a post about going through it. I offered a promissory note to elaborate on what precisely 'it' is at some point in the future. This week, I redeem that note, though not quite in full. 'It' was a breakup. Not entirely of my choosing. The details are messy, overly complicated for my purposes here. I do my best to be a philosopher, or at the very least a passable essayist, rather than a gossip columnist. (Given the amount my relationships have come up in these posts, however, I'm not sure whether I've been terribly successful.)

It's the first time in a few years that I haven't been the one to unilaterally end the relationship, though I'm not sure how much it really matters. When ending a relationship, one can take solace in the fact that they made the best choice they could given their feelings at the time. But even when on the receiving end, one can take solace in the fact that a partner that doesn't want to be with you, even if only for the present moment, isn't an ideal partner anyways.

Either strategy can make the relational transition easier, but, except in extreme cases, not easy. In a long-term, committed relationship, one orients one's life around another person. When that person leaves, so much has to be readjusted. Not only must the daily habits shift, but also the inner narrative of one's life.

That narrative has to adjust to the new past ("What did this relationship mean to me? How does it affect my new present?"), the new present ("Who am I outside of this relationship? How do I approach the world?"), and the new future ("What do I want to bring into my life now that this relationship is gone? What does the future look like without my former partner?"). It is no small task to find a narrative that works, while still, in good faith, negotiating with reality. It is easy to find narratives that pander to the lesser angels of our nature, but these narratives often fail to track what's really out there in the world. 

I could look back at my recently severed relationship and start to nitpick all the little things that would annoy me, all the times my former partner would forget (or "forget") to wear her retainer and wake me up at three in the morning grinding her teeth, or the time we went to Amsterdam and she refused to eat at any restaurant we passed despite increasingly evident hanger. I could stretch these minor nettles into cartoonish disproportion, into mortal sins. Good riddance! I might say, Let her grind to her heart's content!

But reality, at this point, would rightfully walk away from the negotiating table. Yes, I would have preferred to not be woken up by teeth grinding (a horrible sound, like dental scrapers on a chalkboard), and I would have preferred to have found a restaurant to eat at slightly faster. But our relationship was not defined by these moments. Most of the time we found ourselves laughing about them shortly afterwards.

Even among the narratives on offer that do attempt to interface with reality, it is difficult to choose. Did the relationship end because I didn't ask the right questions? Was it because we had gotten into a routine? Maybe I cooked salmon too often? I could adopt the view that we were already growing apart, or that we wanted different things out of life, or that she was jealous of how much her grandpa liked me. None of these are quite *the* truth, and none of them are *the* cause or even *a* cause (who doesn't like salmon?). They're available as pieces of a story, however, and must be either adopted or dispensed.

As a philosopher, an analytic one at that, I am bound by the norms of my discipline to seek *the* truth. Analytic philosophers, unlike some of our more postmodern colleagues in the continental branch of the discipline, tend not to talk about the truth as relative or malleable. There is one way that reality is, and though it might be experienced subjectively, it exists objectively, independent of us.

Adopting this perspective can make this sort of narrative choice more difficult. The right narrative becomes not the one that makes an effort to represent reality faithfully while making allowances for the sanity of its audience, but rather the one that *exactly* represents reality. Exactly representing reality, unfortunately, would require creating a perfect simulation of reality, which would just be a new reality unto itself. Anytime we attempt to capture reality we can only do so in fragments, and we must always take liberties. I can give you my words, but not my inner world, not the way that it feels to sit here and type this piece up, on a Sunday afternoon, in my bedroom, after having what might be the last conversation I'll ever have with my former partner.

My present days have changed from a roughly consistent routine of traveling to campus for work, going over to her apartment, either cooking dinner or grabbing takeout, finding something to do together or at least in the same room in the evening, and retiring to do it all again the next day, to writing, going to the gym, playing chess or backgammon with my roommate, reading, and retiring alone to do it all again the next day.

There are things I miss and things that I live fine without. I do not care for Sex and the City, and its departure from my life is welcome. Her and I had fun together, and I laugh less on average than I did a few months ago without her in my life. Things are still in flux, new routines are being worn into the daily trail of my life. Joy is a scarce resource and must be assiduously foraged along the path. I'm getting better at finding it as a solo scavenger.

In August I'll be going to Montreal for a week, staying in a hostel, generally bumming around. I'm planning a trip to Germany in March and (global oil prices willing) Japan next summer. If I'm lucky, I'll get an adjunct position teaching in the spring that will free up enough capital so that I can live in Chicago next summer. She was going to come to Chicago for Christmas this year, which I was looking forward to, but there will be things to do, people to see. Easier to spend all of winter break in the city without a girlfriend waiting for me in Rochester.

A therapist of mine in high school used to tell me that life is a grieving process. We are constantly losing things, and we have to adjust to that loss. I disagree here only with the employment of grief. Grief is an emotion that I think is uniquely suited for deaths. It's the end of a story, hopefully after a pleasant denouement, and those characters don't get any more lines. Losing a partner is still a loss, but not so final. The roles can be reprised, the themes can recur. The broad points of the plot are fixed by the hard facts of reality.

The words are yours to choose.
