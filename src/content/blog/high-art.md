Within philosophy, I primarily pursue two areas of research: metaphysics and bioethics. Bioethics is the far more practical of the two, dealing with on-the-ground ethical themes that arise in the practice of healthcare. Metaphysics is abstruse, bordering on the esoteric. The sorts of questions I cover in metaphysics include the nature and existence of properties and the analysis of paradoxes. Not the sort of thing that readily lends itself to direct quality of life improvements.

My pursuit of the latter occasionally gives me cause for guilt. In bioethics, I can have a measurable effect on the well-being of actual human patients. For now, this impact is limited to papers and policy memos which aim to influence real-world practice. But if I were to pursue a career in bioethics, perhaps dissertating in it or getting a bioethics certificate on the way to my Ph.D., I could be at the bedside helping providers and patients navigate complex ethical dilemmas.

The work is enjoyable, stimulating. It requires sustained, concerted, delicate thought to be done well, just like any academic field. Not to mention that jobs in bioethics are far more plentiful than jobs in metaphysics. My background working for one of the largest healthcare technology companies in the world also gives me an edge when dealing with technology-related ethical dilemmas, which proliferate with each new advance in data science and information technology.

Yet I still find myself powerless against the gravity of metaphysics. There are no problems more abstract, more challenging, more thoroughly engrossing for me than those offered by this strange subfield. I plan to dissertate in metaphysics, to spend a considerable chunk of the latter half of my summer working on questions about relational properties with my advisor, to continue to pursue the subject like a mad dog.

Metaphysics is not detached from reality. Its task, at least as I see it, is to limn the basic structure of the world. It is, necessarily, concerned with how the world is. Some of the questions of metaphysics have real consequences for the way that we form scientific theories, and some questions about the conditions under which a person remains self-identical are relevant to moral theorizing. It's not, however, going to lead to a cure for cancer or an end to global conflict. In many ways, metaphysics is high art for high art's sake.

Plenty of pursuits are high art for its own sake. Oil painting, couture, macrame. I don't mean to suggest these pursuits are trivial or without value. It is a good to have parts of our society dedicated to creating objects of beauty. Aesthetic value is produced and, with an appropriately well-funded public arts program, available to be enjoyed by the broader public.

Metaphysics, of course, is unlike these pursuits because it does not aim at producing beauty, but rather truth. Metaphysicians want to know what the world is like at its fundament, they do not aim to produce aesthetically pleasing treatises. Anyone who has attempted to read contemporary papers in metaphysics can attest to this. 

Furthermore, metaphysics, done at its highest level, is not intended for public consumption. Papers written in metaphysics have a rather narrow audience of specialists. What little communication work there is to teach metaphysics to those outside this narrow audience mostly occurs within universities, and even then mostly at elite universities. Few community colleges have enough philosophers, or students interested in taking philosophy classes, to offer anything more than the occasional survey course in metaphysics.

Part of me wants to claim that the truth is its own reward. There is something we gain, collectively, as a species, when any one of us discovers something about the way the world is that was previously unknown. Lots of academic fields pursue the same sorts of discoveries. The bleeding edge of theoretical mathematics will, like metaphysics, often have very little immediate practical benefits. But we still put money into theoretical mathematics, and we still value mathematicians as part of our society.

But another part of me just isn't convinced by that. There are certainly truths that aren't worth pursuing. There is a fact of the matter about how many grains of sand are on Miami beach. It would be an impressive logistical and organizational task to precisely count all of those grains of sand (once we settle on the perhaps even more daunting task of determining where the beach begins and ends) and to settle on a precise number. But surely this would be a silly truth to pursue. I wouldn't be inspired by the human pursuit of truth were we to engage in this activity.

Metaphysics is certainly less trivial (usually) than counting grains of sand on the beach. The questions pursued by metaphysicians are profound and novel, and metaphysicians take the work seriously. One cannot make a career of nonsense. I can take at least some solace in that. It is comforting not to contribute to the constant deluge of bullshit, of analysis and commentary that has no regard for the truth, that has become the defining feature of contemporary public discourse.

I think often about the intellectuals of Helen DeWitt's *The Last Samurai*. Some of them are engaged in the sort of pragmatic, charitable work that directly improves, or attempts to improve, the situations of people in the worst straits imaginable. Others are engaged in the sort of high art for high art's sake that I have been wrestling with. DeWitt makes no judgment between the two. The intellectual life, for her, seems to be a matter of self-actualization. The life of the mind is pursued not quite for its own sake, but for the personal fulfillment that its adherents reap.

The entire exercise of writing this piece is, in the end, perhaps a bit silly. At worst, it might even be accused of performative virtue-signaling. I have no intent to change the subfield of my dissertation, and I have no intention to stop pursuing metaphysics. I can feel better about chasing personal fulfillment than engaging in unapologetic intellectual onanism. Maybe next week will be another installment of public philosophy on this blog. If I had definitive answers this week, I'd record them here. Instead, I'm going to leave it here and find something to read.
